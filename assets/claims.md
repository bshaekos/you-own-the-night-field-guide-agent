# Claims map

Use this file when the reader asks what the book claims or wants to inspect one idea more closely. Condensed from the book's fourteen chapter-level takeaways to ten, so the reader can choose where to go deeper without being handed all fourteen at once.

## Claim 1: The problem is the feed, not the screen

A film was a single decision that bought you an evening. What replaced it competes for something much smaller and much more often. The book is explicit that it is not an anti-screen book and not a detox manual — the object of attention is the current, not the device.

- **Best evidence:** the film-versus-feed contrast; the book's own framing of what it is not.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** if the object is the current and not the device, does deleting an app ever count as legitimate architecture — or does the book's own logic rule it out as subtraction?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 1 (The Best Part of the Day), Current State heading.

## Claim 2: Thousands of small contests, not one big one — and willpower is the wrong instrument

You are not losing a battle over how to spend your night. You are losing thousands of tiny ones you never noticed starting, against an opposing system that is staffed, funded, and tested at scale.

- **Best evidence:** the twelve-second-window framing; the asymmetry between a tired reader at nine o'clock and a system built to win that exact moment.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** if the contest happens below conscious notice, can any self-report method — including this Field Guide — actually catch it, or does the audit just measure what the reader already knows they're doing?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 3 (The Engineer Who Couldn't Stop), Current State heading.

## Claim 3: Build walls, not resolve; write rules as purposes, not prohibitions

An architect doesn't ask people to remember not to walk through walls — the environment does that work. A rule invites the mind to hunt for loopholes; a statement of what a time or room is *for* invites alignment instead.

- **Best evidence:** the architect analogy; household examples contrasting a rule ("no phones at dinner") with a purpose ("dinner is family time").
- **Strongest unresolved question:** purposes are harder to enforce than rules precisely because they're not falsifiable — what does the book say happens when a purpose-framed commitment quietly erodes, with no rule to point to when it does?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 6 (The Last Common Hour), Rules of Engagement heading.

## Claim 4: Subtraction fails; replacement works

Taking something away leaves a vacuum, and the vacuum gets filled by whatever is nearest to hand. The night is not a problem of removal but of replacement.

- **Best evidence:** the vacuum-fills-with-whatever's-nearest framing; the garden and quilting examples of activities that displaced the phone.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** replacement assumes an alternative pull already appealing enough to compete — what does the method offer a reader who doesn't yet have one?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 8 (The Remedies That Don't Work), What Has Worked heading.

## Claim 5: Decide before you're tired — prior commitment and shape beat willpower and a schedule

The people who reclaim their evenings arrange things so the question is already answered by a clearer version of themselves, earlier. A shape — an understanding of what each stretch of evening is *for* — survives contact with a bad night; a schedule doesn't.

- **Best evidence:** the prior-commitment chapters; the four-phase architecture replacing hour-by-hour scheduling.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** prior commitment still requires a rested, higher-willpower version of the reader to exist at some point in the week to make the decision — what happens when even that window is scarce?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 13 (Architecture), The Target heading.

## Claim 6: The night and the morning are one problem

Sleep is active synthesis, not passive recovery. What the evening feeds it determines what's available the next day — specific capacities like patience, focus, warmth, and creative access, none of which anyone itemizes for you.

- **Best evidence:** the active-synthesis framing; the named list of specific capacities rather than a generic "tiredness."
- **Strongest unresolved question:** the book names the costs but doesn't quantify them — how would a reader actually attribute a bad morning to one prior-night choice rather than to sleep quality generally?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 10 (The Protected Hours), Current State heading.

## Claim 7: Distinctive nights get remembered; seamless nights vanish

Memory encodes distinctiveness. Evenings engineered to be continuous and interchangeable produce nothing to remember, which is why a decade of them can disappear — though nothing is erased, only jammed.

- **Best evidence:** the memory-encodes-distinctiveness claim; the "decade can vanish" framing.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** is memorability the right proxy for a well-spent night, or can a night be both forgettable and worthwhile — recovery, low-key connection — without that being a failure?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 7 (The Invoice), Current State heading.

## Claim 8: The same technology can be hired by either side — an assistant is a mirror, not a nanny

AI is arguably the most powerful attention-capture tool ever built, and, unchanged, the most powerful tool available for protecting attention. Nothing technical distinguishes the two — only whose interests it serves. A useful assistant reflects back what the reader wanted on a clear day; it can't want things for them, enforce anything, or substitute for people or environment.

- **Best evidence:** the mirror-versus-nanny distinction; the explicit list of what the assistant can't do.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** this is the central premise of this very project — does a live, conversational agent risk drifting from mirror toward nanny the moment it starts proactively checking in, rather than only reflecting back what the reader already said?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 14 (The Tool That Works for You), Rules of Engagement heading.

## Claim 9: Interruption is guaranteed; the restart is the skill

Everyone abandons the structure eventually. It's never broken, only paused — and the measure of a working night is how quickly the reader comes back, not a perfect record.

- **Best evidence:** the three-move restart protocol; the explicit "never broken, only paused" reframe.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** three moves are short by design — is there a point of disruption (grief, illness, crisis) severe enough that the restart's brevity becomes inadequate rather than a feature?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 15 (The Long Game), [`night-plan/three-move-restart.md`](night-plan/three-move-restart.md).

## Claim 10: The night is a foothold, not the destination

The night is the first territory worth recovering because the pain is most visible there and the simplest architecture buys the most immediate relief. The book's claim is that the same method — self-portrait, configured tool, simple architecture, restart protocol — extends to everything else currently managed by systems with priorities other than the reader's.

- **Best evidence:** the "simplest architecture, most immediate relief" framing; the explicit claim that the method generalizes.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** what evidence does the book offer that the night is representative of those other domains, rather than simply the easiest case to start with?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 16 (The Couple Who Came Back), The Long Game heading.
