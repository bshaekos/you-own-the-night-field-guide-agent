# Claims map

*Use this file when the reader asks what the book claims or wants to inspect one idea more closely. Ten claims, in my own voice, each anchored to the chapter and person who taught it to me. I've tried to give you enough to know where to dig, not enough to replace the digging. — J.B.*

## Claim 1: The problem is the feed, not the screen

I was in pictures once. We competed for your evening the honest way — with a film. You made one decision, sat in the dark two hours, and walked out still arguing about what you'd seen. What replaced that isn't the old business with new machinery. It's a different business, and it's not after your evening anymore. It's after your next twelve seconds. I'm not asking you to throw the phone in a lake. Keep the screens. Watch the current.

- **Best evidence:** the film-versus-feed contrast in the Introduction; Tom and Ellen Reyes's side-by-side couch in Chapter One, two screens, two private universes, three feet apart.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** if the object is the current and not the device, does deleting an app ever count as legitimate architecture — or does the book's own logic rule that out as subtraction rather than replacement?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 1 (The Best Part of the Day), Current State heading.

## Claim 2: Willpower is the wrong instrument

Tom Reyes thought he was losing a contest of resolve. He wasn't. He was losing a structural contest against engineers who'd tested six seconds, then twenty, then settled on twelve — the narrowest window in which a tired adult reliably can't decline the next episode. His brother said it best, over a beer: Tom wasn't weak. He was outgunned. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole argument.

- **Best evidence:** the twelve-second autoplay window in Chapter One; Sarah Okafor's catalogue of failed willpower systems in Chapter Eight — the app that locks you out, the digital sabbath she's observed four times in two years.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** if the contest happens below conscious notice, can any self-report method — including the Field Guide itself — actually catch it, or does the audit only measure what the reader already knows they're doing?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 1 (The Best Part of the Day); Ch. 3 (The Engineer Who Couldn't Stop), Current State heading.

## Claim 3: Build walls, not resolve

An architect doesn't trust people to remember not to walk through walls. The walls do that work. Tom and Ellen didn't need more discipline. They needed different walls — a bowl on the counter, forty-five minutes, nothing clever about it. You cannot talk anyone into this. You can only build it and let them notice.

- **Best evidence:** the architect analogy and "the first forty-five" in Chapter One; the Brennan family's shelf by the kitchen door in Chapter Six, parked rather than confiscated.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** a purpose is harder to enforce than a rule, precisely because it isn't falsifiable — what happens when a purpose-framed commitment quietly erodes, with no rule left to catch it?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 1 (The Best Part of the Day); Ch. 6 (The Last Common Hour), Rules of Engagement heading.

## Claim 4: Subtraction fails; replacement works

Take something away and you leave a vacuum, and the vacuum gets filled by whatever's nearest to hand — which, for most of us, is the device we just put down. Sarah Okafor tried every subtractive remedy there is. None of them held. What held was a pottery class and a novel moved onto the coffee table. She told me the sentence I've since quoted more than any other in this book: she didn't stop wanting to scroll. She just gave herself something better to do.

- **Best evidence:** Sarah Okafor's full account in Chapter Eight; Robert Hale learning to cook in Chapter Nine, not to fix anything, only because he was divorced and hungry.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** replacement assumes an alternative pull already appealing enough to compete — what does the book actually offer a reader who doesn't have one yet?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 8 (The Remedies That Don't Work), What Has Worked heading.

## Claim 5: Decide before you're tired

Priya Shah spent a year trying to be a better mother at nine p.m. and failing, because by nine p.m. the part of her that could choose had gone offline for the day. What worked was a notecard, written at seven in the morning by a clearer version of herself — three lines, carried in a pocket, deciding the night before the night could talk her out of it. The people who reclaim their evenings don't win at nine o'clock. They arrange things so the question's already answered.

- **Best evidence:** Priya Shah's notecard in Chapter Four; Maeve Sullivan's four-phase shape replacing her refrigerator chart in Chapter Thirteen.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** prior commitment still requires a rested version of the reader to exist at some point in the week to make the decision — what happens when even that window is scarce?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 4 (The Parent Who Disappeared); Ch. 13 (Architecture), The Target heading.

## Claim 6: The night and the morning are one problem

Nina Castellanos writes fiction between six and eight every morning, and for years she didn't understand that those two hours were downstream of what she'd fed her brain the night before. Sleep isn't passive recovery. It's active synthesis — the mind working on what you gave it. She'd been, in her own words, tending the crop and neglecting the soil.

- **Best evidence:** Nina Castellanos's full account in Chapter Ten — the pantry she'd stocked with junk; Tom Reyes's unexpected improvement in sleep in Chapter One, a benefit he hadn't been looking for.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** the book names the costs — patience, focus, warmth, creative access — but doesn't quantify them. How would a reader actually attribute one bad morning to one prior-night choice, rather than to sleep quality in general?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 10 (The Protected Hours), Current State heading.

## Claim 7: Distinctive nights get remembered; seamless nights vanish

David Mercer, recovering from a cardiac event, couldn't produce the narrative of his own recent nights — a man who'd spent his career reconstructing other people's timelines for juries, unable to account for his own evenings. Memory encodes distinctiveness. An evening engineered to be continuous and interchangeable is engineered, by the same stroke, to erase itself. Nothing was lost that couldn't be recovered. The screen hadn't erased him. It had only jammed the signal.

- **Best evidence:** David Mercer's hospital stay in Chapter Seven — "the invoice came due."
- **Strongest unresolved question:** is memorability really the right measure of a well-spent night, or can a night be both forgettable and worthwhile — recovery, low-key connection — without that being a failure?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 7 (The Invoice), Current State heading.

## Claim 8: An assistant is a mirror, not a nanny

James Chen — who spent six years building the very systems this book is about — tried pointing an AI assistant at himself to police his own nights. He lasted four days. It nagged, and nagging produces exactly the resistance the platforms already exploit. What worked was the opposite instruction: not to scold, but to reflect back what he'd said he wanted on a clearer day. The tools are the same ones that captured your attention in the first place. The only thing that changes is the client.

- **Best evidence:** James Chen's full account in Chapter Fourteen, including his own description of what the tool can't do — can't want things for you, can't enforce, can't substitute for other people.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** this is the central premise of this very project. Does a live, conversational agent risk drifting from mirror toward nanny the moment it starts proactively checking in, rather than only reflecting back what the reader already said?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 14 (The Tool That Works for You), Rules of Engagement heading.

## Claim 9: Interruption is guaranteed; the restart is the skill

Marcus Hill built his evening structure in three weeks, then lost it for six after one work trip. The second time it took three weeks to come back. By the time I met him it took a day, sometimes two. He didn't get better at not falling off. He got better at the return — three moves, short, concrete, runnable on any night, regardless of what kind of night it's been. The structure is never broken. Only paused.

- **Best evidence:** Marcus Hill's three-move restart in Chapter Fifteen; my own version of it, which I'll admit to using more often than I'd like.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** three moves are short by design — is there a point of disruption severe enough (grief, illness, real crisis) that the restart's brevity becomes inadequate rather than a feature?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 15 (The Long Game).

## Claim 10: The night is a foothold, not the destination

Tom and Ellen Reyes, two years on, hadn't just gotten their evenings back. They'd gotten themselves back — a woodworking bench, a small business neither of them had planned for. You don't know, before you reclaim the night, what will grow in the space that opens. Nobody does. The night is the first territory worth recovering because the pain is most visible there and the simplest architecture buys the most immediate relief. But it's a foothold. The same method extends to everything else currently being run by systems with priorities other than yours.

- **Best evidence:** the Reyeses' second visit in Chapter Sixteen; Eleanor Price discovering, in Chapter Twelve, that retirement had taken away a structure she'd never known she depended on.
- **Strongest unresolved question:** what evidence does the book actually offer that the night is representative of those other domains, rather than simply the easiest case to start with?
- **Where to look:** Ch. 16 (The Couple Who Came Back), The Long Game heading.
