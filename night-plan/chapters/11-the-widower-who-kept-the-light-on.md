## Summary

- Harold Bennett's wife died on a Wednesday in March, after forty-nine years of marriage. For Harold, the night wasn't a problem of productivity. It was a problem of survival, clarifying something the book's younger subjects cannot.
- For the first eight months he kept the television on, even in other rooms, because the silence had become unbearable — the house had always held two voices, and now one was gone. His daughter was the one who finally asked him, plainly, what he actually did with his nights, and what she heard was something harder to name than grief. She told him, gently, that he'd been not so much grieving as disappearing.
- What his recovery actually looked like — recovery isn't quite the right word for it — started with one phone call a night, to one person, for no particular reason except to be in a conversation. What he came to understand about quiet and noise is the sentence of his the book returns to more than any other.

## The Four Questions
