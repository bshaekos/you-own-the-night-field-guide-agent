## Summary

- Maeve Sullivan can tell you, to the half-hour, where four hundred nurses are supposed to be on any day for the next six weeks. It's her job, and she's extraordinary at it. She could not hold a single Tuesday night of her own — and when she tried to solve it the way she solves everything, with a chart on the refrigerator, it survived about four days.
- The chapter names the mistake nearly everyone makes trying to take the night back: treating it as a scheduling problem. A schedule is a list of appointments, and the first time real life disagrees with the clock, the schedule loses and the whole project goes in the trash with the chart. What Maeve found instead — what nearly everyone in the book who rebuilt a working night eventually found, in one version or another — was a shape. Four phases, each one feeding the next: the coming-down, the part that's actually hers, the powering-off, and sleep.
- Each phase gets its own small fix, including the almost insultingly simple one for the first phase and the single piece of furniture that made the second one stick. Maeve's own line about the difference between a schedule and a shape is one of the most useful sentences in the book.

## The Four Questions

| Four Questions | |
|---|---|
| **Audit** | Design your ideal night across the four phases — coming-down, the part that's yours, powering-off, sleep. |
| **Cost** | Which phase, fixed, would change the most? |
| **Swap** | Rebuild one phase this week — most people start with the coming-down. |
| **Check** | Did giving the night a shape do what charts and schedules couldn't? |
