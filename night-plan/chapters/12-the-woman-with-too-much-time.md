## Summary

- Eleanor Price retired at sixty-eight after forty-two years teaching elementary school, and had made lists, for a decade, of everything she'd do with her freed-up hours. In none of her lists had she accounted for the evenings — a common oversight, and one most people make without noticing they've made it.
- Her evenings, it turned out, had depended invisibly on the structure of her days. When the days emptied out, the nights didn't stay the same. They lost their shape, and a retirement phone — a genuinely different animal from a working person's phone, the chapter argues — moved in to fill the space.
- What changed things for Eleanor was a former student, now a grown man, writing to thank her for something she'd said to him at eleven that she doesn't even remember saying. What she did with that correspondence is the clearest illustration in the book of a principle worth stating outright: retirement isn't the issue. Structure is the issue, at any age.
