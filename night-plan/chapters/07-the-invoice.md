## Summary

- David Mercer spent thirty years as a trial attorney, reconstructing other people's timelines for juries on demand — who was where, who said what, on the evening of March fourteenth. After a cardiac event put him in a hospital bed for four forced days of rest, he discovered he couldn't produce the narrative of his own recent nights. They had happened to him, not with him.
- The chapter is about memory, directly — why entire years of evenings get forgotten while single nights from decades ago don't. Experience gets encoded by distinctiveness, and an evening engineered to be continuous and seamless is engineered, by the same stroke, to erase itself as it unfolds.
- What Mercer found on the far side of four uncomfortable days wasn't transcendence. It was something more ordinary, and more valuable, and it came with a notebook he started keeping at the kitchen table at eleven at night. His own phrase for the whole experience — quoted more than almost anything else in the book — is the chapter's closing line.
