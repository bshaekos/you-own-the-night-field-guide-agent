# The Three-Move Restart

## Why There Is a Restart at All

Behavioral-change research separates two problems.

**Implementation** is the initial adoption of a new behavior — mostly a design challenge: make the new behavior easier than the old one. That's the work the Field Guide does.

**Maintenance** is sustaining it through disruption — a resilience challenge, and it needs a different tool.

The interruptions come for everyone: a work crisis, a family emergency, a vacation, a stretch of illness or grief or fatigue that turns the structure into one more thing to manage rather than a thing that supports you.

> The question is not whether you'll abandon the structure. You will, at some point. The question is how quickly you come back.

## The Protocol

> When the structure breaks — and it will — do these three moves, in this order. Nothing else counts that first night back.

| Move | What it does |
|---|---|
| **1.** Phone in the kitchen. | Removes the strongest pull before anything else is attempted. |
| **2.** A fifteen-minute walk, no phone. | Resets the nervous system; breaks the spell of the disrupted period. |
| **3.** A book on the couch when you come back. | Provides the alternative path the moment you sit down. |

Run in any other order, the moves work against each other — a book on the couch with the phone still in your pocket isn't a restart, it's an intention.

## The Three Properties

A restart protocol only holds if it survives the conditions that made the reader need it:

- **Short** — no more than three moves. A five-move protocol read on a bad Tuesday is another thing to fail at.
- **Concrete** — no vague intentions, only actions. The test: could someone else watch and say whether it was done?
- **Runnable on any night**, regardless of circumstance. Build for the worst version of the evening, not the average one.

## Writing a Reader's Own Restart Moves

Ask these when helping a reader write their three moves under Rules of Engagement:

1. **Clear the field.** What's the single strongest pull in their evening, and where does it physically go?
2. **Change state.** What can they do in fifteen minutes, indoors or out, in any weather, that ends the day rather than continuing it?
3. **Supply the alternative.** What's waiting for them when they sit back down — already open, requiring no decision?

Check each move against the three properties above, then against the worst night the reader can reasonably expect. If it survives that night, it survives the others.
